Mushen family history:400 years of American beginnings.

They did not arrive once. They arrived in five separate waves, from five different places, across two and a half centuries — and the name at the top of this page belonged to the last people through the door.

Part One · 1620–1640 · 472 people

The Great Migration

Twenty thousand English Puritans left for New England in twenty years. It was not a trickle of adventurers; it was the organised evacuation of a religious minority, and it emptied whole parishes.

I

They left a church they could not reform and a king who would not let them try. Where they came from is the signature of the thing: overwhelmingly the eastern counties, the heart of English Puritanism, which exported it wholesale. A second, smaller stream came out of the West Country.

Three hundred and twenty-six of them died in Massachusetts Bay Colony and fifty-seven in Plymouth. A further eight were born in Leiden — children of the Separatist congregation that fled to Holland in 1608, spent twelve years in exile, and then crossed. Their birthplaces are Dutch. They were English refugees twice over.

Nine were aboard the Mayflower itself, and half the ship's company was dead within four months. What the survivors then did occupies the next century and a half: they founded towns that still exist, fought the first full-scale war between colonists and Native nations, and hanged two sisters at Salem on the evidence of children.

Then they stopped moving. Five straight generations were born in America and married other colonists in the same handful of towns. The family crossed the Atlantic once, together, and stayed put for a hundred and fifty years.

Crossing I · the Mayflower

John Howland

c. 1592–1673 · Fenstanton, Huntingdonshire · manservant

He was not a Separatist and not a gentleman — he was somebody's servant, which is the only reason he was aboard. Mid-Atlantic, in a gale, he came up on deck and was thrown over the side. He caught hold of a topsail halyard trailing in the water, held on while the ship dragged him under and out again, and was hauled back aboard with a boat hook. He then outlived nearly everyone, married Elizabeth Tilley — who had been orphaned in the first winter at thirteen — and had ten children who all survived to adulthood. For the period that outcome is almost unheard of, and it is the reason his descendants now number in the millions.

“…and though he was something ill with it, yet he lived many years after.”William Bradford, Of Plymouth Plantation

Part Two · 1624–1664 · 16 people

New Netherland

While the Puritans were filling Massachusetts, a second set of ancestors landed two hundred miles south, under a different flag, speaking a different language, and built the town that became New York.

II

Utrecht, Amsterdam, Amersfoort, Hoorn, Arnhem — and Valenciennes in Hainaut, which is the tell. The first settlers the Dutch West India Company put ashore in 1624 were not Dutch at all but Walloons: French-speaking Protestant refugees who had fled the Spanish Netherlands and were living in Holland as exiles when the Company offered them land.

Ghislain de la Vigne and Adrienne Cuvellier, both born at Valenciennes, were among that first cohort in New Amsterdam; he was dead by 1632, in the settlement's opening years.

Wolfert Gerritse van Couwenhoven came out from Amersfoort and helped establish the Brooklyn town of Flatlands — known then as New Amersfoort, named for the place he had left. Nicasius de Sille, a lawyer from Arnhem, arrived in 1653 as a councillor to Peter Stuyvesant and was among the founders of New Utrecht.

The English took the colony in 1664 without a shot. The Dutch families simply stayed and became English subjects — which is why these death places read Brooklyn, Bushwick, Flatbush, Flatlands and New Utrecht. Dutch villages that are now neighbourhoods, still carrying their names.

Part Three · 1709–1760 · 21 people

The Palatines

The winter of 1708 was the coldest in five hundred years. Wine froze in the barrel. It came down on a Rhineland already wrecked by decades of French invasion, and it broke the region.

III

Around thirteen thousand people went down the Rhine to Rotterdam and on to London in 1709, camping in fields outside the city while Queen Anne's government decided what to do with them. Britain shipped roughly three thousand to New York to make tar and pitch for the Royal Navy.

The scheme failed within two years. The Palatines walked west into the Mohawk Valley and took the land instead. That is exactly where these ancestors end up — Klock, Dygert, Fuchs, Etel and Riemensnyder all die at Palatine, Stone Arabia, German Flatts, Schoharie and Herkimer.

Which put them, two generations on, in the worst possible place. In August 1777 the Tryon County militia walked into an ambush at Oriskany — among the bloodiest engagements of the war by proportion of men lost, fought at close quarters against Loyalist neighbours.

Part Four · 1718–1775 · 12 people

The Ulster Scots

Scottish twice removed: Lowland Presbyterians planted in the north of Ireland in the 1600s, who left again a century later when Ireland turned out to be no better.

IV

County Londonderry gave the Gilmores, County Donegal the Borlands, County Antrim the Templetons, County Down the Campbells. They left over rack-rents, tithes owed to a church they did not attend, and laws that barred Presbyterians from public office.

Almost to a person they landed in Pennsylvania — Cumberland, Franklin, Westmoreland, Mercer and Armstrong counties, the Scots-Irish corridor running down the back of the Appalachians. The one exception proves it: William Jamison died at Windham, New Hampshire, next door to Londonderry, New Hampshire, a town founded in 1719 by emigrants from Ulster and named for the place they came from.

The Gilmores were Covenanters — Presbyterians whose refusal to accept bishops or royal authority over the church had got them killed in Scotland and planted in Ireland. The family story says Robert Gilmore and his brother were permitted to emigrate but their two sisters were not, so the brothers smuggled them aboard and hid them in the hold until the ship was far enough out that turning back was not worth the captain's trouble.

No ship has been identified and eighteenth-century passenger lists are patchy. It is recorded here as tradition. But it belongs to a real migration, and the family it describes did land in Pennsylvania and did produce a Continental Marine.

Crossing I · Salem, 1692

Rebecca Nurse

1621–1692 · Great Yarmouth, Norfolk · hanged 19 July 1692

She was seventy-one, devout, and well enough regarded that thirty-nine of her neighbours signed a petition on her behalf — an extraordinary act in that atmosphere, and a dangerous one. The jury returned a verdict of not guilty. The court sent them back to reconsider, and they came back with a conviction. She was hanged on Gallows Hill in July. Her sister Mary Esty was hanged the following September; a third sister, Sarah Cloyce, was imprisoned and survived. Three sisters, one accusation, and this line descends through all three families. The spellings vary between Towne, Towen and Town, because that is how the clerks wrote it.

“I can say before my Eternal Father I am innocent, and God will clear my innocency.”Rebecca Nurse, at her examination, 1692

Part Five · 1840–1870 · 8 people

The Industrial Crossing

Two hundred and twenty years after the Mayflower, with the family long since American, eight more people got on ships. They were not fleeing a bishop or a king. They were going where the work was.

V

Kilmarnock in Ayrshire sent Robert Linton and Elizabeth Smith, out of the Scottish weaving belt. Ireland sent Hugh Linton, Agnes Mitchell and Alexander Campbell. England sent one man.

They did not go to farmland. They went to Pawtucket and Providence, Rhode Island — the birthplace of American industry, where Samuel Slater had built the country's first water-powered cotton mill in 1793 — and to Worcester, Massachusetts. Ayrshire weavers turning up in a mill town is not a coincidence; it is a career move. The others went into coal and iron in Armstrong County, Pennsylvania.

Meanwhile a different branch was going the other way. In 1852 the Gores drove an ox wagon from Iowa over the Oregon Trail into the Rogue River Valley, built a house, put a stockade around it during the wars of the 1850s, planted the orchard above, and patented a wind wheel.

And here is the part worth sitting with. Of the 544 people in this record who crossed an ocean, the one who carried the surname at the top of the page was among the last eight to arrive. Albert Mushen was born in England in 1840 and died at Worcester in 1917 — four generations back. Nine ancestors were already standing at Plymouth in 1620. Two hundred and twenty years of Warners, Gores, Nyes, Townes and Howlands had founded towns, been hanged, fought two wars and buried each other in New England ground before anyone in this family was called Mushen at all.

A surname travels down one thread out of thousands. On this page, the name is the newest thing in the building.

Crossing V · Oregon

Emerson Elijah Gore

1824–1910 · Vermont → Iowa → Oregon · farmer, inventor

He crossed the plains by ox wagon in 1852 with his wife Mary Elizabeth Gilmore — a Covenanter's granddaughter, which is how two of these five crossings finally meet in a single marriage — and settled in the Rogue River Valley. He built the house, fenced it against the wars of the 1850s, cleared the orchard, and took out a patent. He is also the first person in four hundred years of this record whose face can simply be looked at, rather than inferred from a will, a muster roll or a gravestone. Everything before him is documents.

Where this comes from. 24,263 ancestors traced through the FamilySearch collaborative tree, walked outward generation by generation and stored locally. Kinship was established from the tree's own parent–child edges, not by matching names — which is how nine Mayflower passengers were confirmed and two further candidates rejected: a William Brewster who died in 1593 when the Elder lived until 1644, and a George Soule born a century and a half too early to have boarded anything.

How claims are graded. Facts carried by two or more independent archives are marked documented. Everything else is either supported by a single record or recorded plainly as family tradition, and is labelled as such where it appears. Spellings are given as the clerk wrote them; corrections belong in the note, not in the record.

On the images. There is one photographic family in this record, and it is the last one. The first four crossings left no likenesses at all — the plates for those centuries are emblems and nineteenth-century engravings made by people who never saw their subjects, and they are captioned as such. Nothing here is presented as a portrait that is not one.
