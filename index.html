Mushen family history:
400 years of American beginnings.

A visual history of how one family became American over four centuries.

Nine of them sailed on the Mayflower. One founded a town after a quarrel about baptism. Two sisters were hanged at Salem. Five fought in the Revolution. One drove an ox wagon into Oregon and built a fort around his house. Same family, the whole way through, and the name at the top of this page belonged to the last people through the door.

Inland · 1620–1900

The inland migration: Plymouth to Oregon

Crossing the Atlantic was the short part. Once ashore, this family spent the next two hundred and thirty years moving west across the continent, and because every ancestor is dated and placed, you can watch it happen generation by generation.

The band below is every ancestor with a known American death place, one column per generation, running from the deepest on the left to the most recent on the right. Colour is where they died.

For six straight generations, New England holds between 85% and 95%. These people landed in Massachusetts and simply stayed — marrying other colonists in the same handful of towns, for a century and a half.

Then look at the step between the eighth generation and the seventh. New England collapses from 85% to 39% in a single generation. Nothing about that is gradual. It is the Revolution: Congress finished the war with no money and a great deal of western land, and paid its soldiers in acres.

And it is the same generation on both sides of that fact. The seventh generation has a median birth year of 1750 — twenty-five years old at Lexington. Ebenezer Nye was born in 1750 and Ebenezer Porter in 1732. The generation that fought is the generation that left. They were paid in land, and they went and stood on it.

The second rule on the chart is the Civil War. The fourth generation has a median birth year of 1841, which put its men at twenty when the war began — Albert Mushen was twenty-one, Robert Linton twenty, Lyman Beecher Warner twenty-four, Alexander Campbell thirty-three. Eleven men of the right age, spread across Massachusetts, Rhode Island, Pennsylvania and Oregon. Not one of them carries a service record in this tree, which is a gap and not a finding. It means nobody has looked, not that nobody went.

But the Civil War did not send them west

It is the obvious guess, and the dates refuse it. The West appears on this chart at the fourth generation, but the family reached Oregon on 27 September 1852, nine years before Fort Sumter. Whatever moved them, the war had not happened yet.

What moved them was the same thing that moved them the first time: the federal government giving away land. In 1788 it was military warrants — Congress finished the Revolution with no money and paid its soldiers in Ohio acres, and the seventh generation went and stood on them. In 1850 it was the Donation Land Claim Act, signed by President Fillmore on 27 September 1850, which granted 320 acres to a single man and 640 to a married couple who reached Oregon Territory and worked it.

That is why the family record calls their land in the Rogue River Valley the Gore Donation Claim. It is a legal term, and it is the reason they went. Emerson and Mary Gore left Iowa in April 1852, nineteen months after the Act passed, and arrived on 27 September — two years to the day after it was signed, which is a coincidence but a tidy one.

So: both jumps west were land policy, not war. The wars decided who was eligible.

After that New England never comes back. The Mid-Atlantic peaks at 59%, the Midwest at 41%, and by the third generation the family's centre of gravity is Oregon — three thousand miles from Plymouth, having taken nine generations to get there.

Part One · 1620–1640 · 488 people

The Great Migration

Twenty thousand English Puritans left for New England in twenty years. It was not a trickle of adventurers; it was the organised evacuation of a religious minority, and it emptied whole parishes.

I

Why they left1629–1640

Nobody crosses an ocean over a theological abstraction. So it is worth being exact about what actually happened to a family in Suffolk, in their own parish, in the 1630s.

Their minister was taken away from them. In 1633 Charles I reissued the Book of Sports — a royal declaration permitting dancing, archery and games on Sunday afternoons, and ordered it read aloud from every pulpit in England. To a Puritan the Sabbath was holy and this was licensed sin, and the order required their own minister to stand in their own church and publish it in their hearing. Ministers who refused were suspended, deprived of their livings and stripped of their licence to preach.

That is the mechanism. The choice put to a parish was to conform or to leave the country, and when the minister went, his congregation went with him. These people did not emigrate as individuals weighing risk. They emigrated as congregations following the man who had been silenced. It is exactly what John Lothropp did with his church, and what Andrew Warner did twice more once he was here.

And the trade that fed them was already dying. The eastern counties lived on cloth, and the cloth trade had been depressed for the better part of twenty years — war with Spain had closed its best market. At Colchester the weekly output of bays fell from four hundred pieces to fifty, with £6,000 of unsold stock sitting in 1629. By 1631 the town's poor were petitioning against wage cuts. The poor were filling up the towns.

So the answer to why risk it is that both halves of a life failed in the same decade and the same county: the man who ran your spiritual world was silenced, and the trade that paid for your bread had collapsed. Set against that, eight weeks at sea was not the frightening part. The frightening part was the first winter ashore, and they knew it, because half of the Mayflower company had already died of it.

Which is why the origins read as they do. Overwhelmingly the eastern counties — Suffolk, Essex, Norfolk — the heart of English Puritanism and the heart of the failing cloth country, the same ground twice over. A second, smaller stream came out of the West Country.

Three hundred and twenty-six of them died in Massachusetts Bay Colony and fifty-seven in Plymouth. A further eight were born in Leiden — children of the Separatist congregation that fled to Holland in 1608, spent twelve years in exile there, and then crossed. Their birthplaces are Dutch. They were English refugees twice over.

This is not a family that trickled over. It is a family that was inside an event. At the twelfth generation back, 96% of known ancestors were born abroad. Four generations later it has collapsed to 1%, and stays there.

Then they stopped moving. Five straight generations were born in America and married other colonists in the same handful of towns. The family crossed the Atlantic once, together, and did not move again for a century and a half.

And the migration itself stopped, almost on a date. In 1640 the Long Parliament met, turned on the King's advisers and impeached Laud, who was eventually beheaded. The grievance evaporated, and the sailings collapsed. That is the proof it was never wanderlust: it had an on-switch and an off-switch, and both were political.

Plymouth · November 1620

Nine of them were on the Mayflower

A hundred and two passengers sailed from Plymouth in September 1620 in a merchant ship built for wine, not people. Nine of them are direct ancestors. Half the company was dead within four months.

James Chiltonc. 1556–1620
Canterbury

A tailor from Canterbury, and at sixty-four the oldest person aboard. He never saw Plymouth. He died on 8 December 1620 while the ship still lay at anchor in Provincetown Harbour, before the settlement existed — the first of the passengers to die in the New World, in sight of it. He had sailed with his wife and their youngest daughter Mary. Of the three, only Mary lived through the winter.

John Howlandc. 1592–1673
Fenstanton, Hunts.

A manservant, not a Separatist. Mid-Atlantic, in a gale, he came up on deck and was thrown overboard. He caught hold of a topsail halyard trailing in the water, held on while the ship dragged him under and out again, and was hauled back aboard with a boat hook. He went on to outlive nearly everyone.

John Tilley1571–1621
Joan Hurst1568–1621

Husband and wife, and both dead by spring. John signed the Mayflower Compact and helped explore the coast for a landing site. Neither survived the first winter, which killed roughly half the company. They left behind a thirteen-year-old daughter.

Elizabeth Tilley1607–1687
Henlow, Beds.

That daughter. Thirteen years old when she sailed, and orphaned in a foreign country before she was fourteen. She survived, married John Howland, the servant who had been pulled out of the sea, and lived to eighty. Nearly every Mayflower descendant in America traces through her.

Dr. Samuel Fuller1580–1633
Redenhall, Norfolk

The colony's physician and surgeon, and a deacon of its church. He brought a servant, William Butten, who died at sea within sight of land — the only passenger lost on the voyage itself. Fuller spent thirteen years doctoring Plymouth and was sent to help Massachusetts Bay through its own first sickness. He died in the epidemic of 1633, still treating people.

Degory Priestc. 1579–1621
Leiden

A Separatist from the Leiden congregation who sailed without his wife and two daughters, meaning to send for them once there was something to send for. He died on 1 January 1621, in the first winter. His widow and daughters reached Plymouth three years later, on the Anne.

Francis Cooke1583–1663
Leiden

Compact signer, and one of the few who lived a long life. His wife Hester and their children followed on the Anne in 1623. Their daughter Jane, born in Leiden during the congregation's Dutch exile, married Experience Mitchell at Plymouth.

Francis Eaton1596–1633
Bristol

A carpenter, baptised at St Thomas's in Bristol, who sailed with his wife Sarah and an infant son. In a colony with no buildings and a winter coming, a carpenter was worth more than a gentleman. He survived that first year; the 1633 sickness took him too.

Keep it in proportion: all nine together are 0.110% of total ancestry. And nine is not nine coincidences — Plymouth was a hundred people, and within two generations everyone had married everyone. What the number really says is that a whole slab of this family was inside the founding.

Crossing I · the man overboard

John Howland, overboard mid-Atlantic

c. 1592–1673 & 1607–1687 · married at Plymouth, c. 1624

Halfway across the Atlantic, in a gale, a servant named John Howland came up on deck and was thrown over the side. He caught a topsail halyard trailing in the water and held on while the ship dragged him under and out again, until they got a boat hook into him and hauled him back aboard. Four berths away sat a thirteen-year-old girl named Elizabeth Tilley, travelling with her mother and father. By the following spring both her parents were dead of that first winter, along with half the ship's company, and she was orphaned in a country with nothing in it.

Three or four years later she married the man who had fallen off the ship. They had ten children, and every one of them survived to adulthood — an outcome so unlikely for the period that it is the single reason their descendants now number in the millions. Four of the nine Mayflower ancestors in this record are that one knot of people: the servant who nearly drowned, the girl he married, and the two parents who did not last the winter.

“…and though he was something ill with it, yet he lived many years after.”William Bradford, Of Plymouth Plantation

Massachusetts Bay · 1630–1653 documented

Gov. Thomas Dudley

1576–1653 · Yardley Hastings, Northants → Roxbury · four times governor

Born 12 October 1576, son of Roger Dudley, a captain in the English army who was killed in action — this tree places that death in Gelderland in 1586, during the English expedition sent to help the Dutch revolt against Spain. Thomas became a soldier himself, serving in the French Wars of Religion under Sir Arthur Savage and fighting the Spanish at the siege of Amiens in 1597. Afterwards he was steward to the Earl of Lincoln, whose Lincolnshire estate was a centre of Nonconformist thought.

He sailed on the Arbella, 8 April 1630, and reached Salem Harbour on 12 June — the Winthrop Fleet, the founding of Massachusetts Bay proper, ten years after Plymouth. He was Governor four times: 1634–35, 1640–41, 1645–46 and 1650–51.

And on 30 May 1650, in that last term, he put his name to the Charter of Harvard College.

The complication

Everyone else in this part of the record is refusing an authority. Lothropp goes to gaol rather than swear the King heads the church. Warner walks out of Hartford. Hadley hides two regicides.

Dudley was the authority. He was harder than Winthrop, and he ruled against Anne Hutchinson in the Antinomian Controversy — the colony's great test of whether a person could differ from its ministers and stay. He was on the side that said no.

And he did not soften. His last written words were verses against religious toleration, reportedly found in his pocket after he died: a warning to men of God to watch over any who would “a toleration hatch,” lest the ill egg bring forth a cockatrice. Toleration had become, to him, the crowning sin. supported

Which is worth leaving in rather than tidying away. A family history that only collects the people who defied power is telling half a story. This one also produced the man holding it.

And his daughter, who is not an ancestor

Anne Bradstreet, Dudley's daughter, was the first poet published in North America. The Tenth Muse Lately Sprung Up in America appeared in London in 1650, while her father was governor.

She is not in this line. The descent runs through her younger sister Mercy Dudley (1621–1691), who married the Rev. John Woodbridge and died at Newbury. So Anne Bradstreet is the sister of a direct ancestor, an aunt, nine generations up, and this page says so plainly, because the ordinary thing for a family history to do at this point is quietly promote her.

That deserves its own sentence, because of what the document turned out to be.

Henry Dunster, Harvard's first president, had asked the General Court for a charter. Dudley endorsed it on 30 May 1650 — the tag at the foot of the document bears his name, and the Court issued it on 9 June. It created a corporate body with perpetual succession, to be called “the President and Fellowes of Haruard Colledge.”

That body still exists. The Harvard Corporation has operated continuously under that charter for three hundred and seventy-six years, which makes it the oldest corporation in the Western Hemisphere — older than every company, every bank and every other university on this side of the Atlantic, and older than the United States by well over a century.

He did not found Harvard; the college dates from 1636. What he signed is the instrument that made it permanent. Three years later he was dead, at Roxbury on 31 July 1653 — the same town where John Gore would die four years after that.

Crossing I · London, 1632 documented

Rev. John Lothropp

1584–1653 · Etton, Yorkshire → Barnstable · two years in prison

He is the best-attested person in this entire record — ninety sources on a tree where most people carry fewer than twenty, and he is here for the plainest reason on the page. He went to prison for what he believed and would not take it back.

Baptised at Etton in the East Riding on 20 December 1584, he went up to Queens' College, Cambridge, and was ordained into the Church of England. In 1623 he renounced his orders and took over an illegal Independent congregation in London — a church that met in private houses because meeting anywhere else was a crime.

On 22 April 1632 the king's officers found them. Forty-two were arrested; eighteen got away. The charge was failure to take the Oath of Supremacy — refusing to acknowledge the Crown as head of the church. He was held for two years.

While he was inside, his wife Hannah fell ill and died. Their six surviving children were left with no parent at liberty and, by tradition, begged for bread in the streets of London.

What happened next is the part worth keeping. The children were taken, dressed in their best, to the Bishop at Lambeth, to stand in front of him and be looked at. The contemporary account says they “made known unto him their miserable condition by reason of their father's being continued in close durance.”

It worked, in the narrowest possible way. He was released on bond in May 1634 and ordered to leave England immediately. He sailed on the Griffin and reached Boston on 18 September 1634. He went first to Scituate and then, on 11 October 1639, led his people to a place they named Barnstable. He died there on 8 November 1653, aged sixty-eight, having buried a wife in a country that gaoled him and founded a town in one that did not.

The pattern this sets

Read the next three sections together and the family's whole first century is one argument, escalating:

Lothropp goes to prison rather than swear that the King is head of the church, 1632. Andrew Warner walks out of Hartford rather than concede a point about baptism, and founds Hadley, 1659. And Hadley then hides two of the men who signed a king's death warrant for twenty-eight years, from 1664.

These are three different people in three different decades, and it is the same refusal each time. It is also, in the end, what the Great Migration was — not an adventure, and not a land grab, but twenty thousand people who would not sign something.

Two honest limits. Which prison is not known. Historians put him in either the Clink or Newgate; the primary documentation does not settle it, and this page will not either. And the children at Lambeth are recorded “according to tradition” — the arrest, the charge, the two years and the terms of release are documented; the scene in front of the Bishop is the family's version of it.

He is claimed as an ancestor by an unusually large number of people, six U.S. presidents among them, which is a fact about seventeenth-century arithmetic rather than about him. Ninety sources are attached to his record because thousands of people have gone looking.

Not an ancestor · the man in the wig

The great-great-grandson

1740–1816 · Minister of the Second Church, Boston, 1768–1816

Here is why a man who died in 1653 keeps turning up in a Georgian periwig. This is Rev. John Lathrop, D.D., and he is John Lothropp's great-great-grandson, born eighty-seven years after him. He held the pulpit of Boston's Second Church, the Mathers' old church, for forty-eight years.

He is a real portrait of a real man. He is simply not the man on the badge's caption. Somewhere along the way a descendant's likeness drifted up the tree and attached itself to the ancestor who shared his name, and once attached, it propagated, because the two are four generations and a spelling apart.

It is worth keeping on the page for exactly that reason. Nearly every wrong portrait in a family history was once a right portrait of somebody else.

The Connecticut Valley · 1636–1660 documented

Andrew Warner founds a town

Some ancestors survived history. This one built it.

Andrew Warner (c. 1595–1684) arrived in Massachusetts Bay in the 1630s, then joined the migration that pushed inland to found Hartford on the Connecticut River. He was an original proprietor — one of the men who held the town's land in the beginning and parcelled it out.

Then, in 1659, he did it again. A doctrinal quarrel split the Hartford and Wethersfield churches — a fight over baptism and church membership bitter enough that the losing side chose to leave rather than submit. They moved twenty-five miles north into the Massachusetts wilderness and founded Hadley.

The town's first town meeting was held at Andrew Warner's house on 8 October 1660. He served as juror, selectman and highway commissioner, and worked as a maltster. Hadley became one of the major towns of the upper Connecticut Valley and the parent settlement of several others.

What the town did nextwinter 1664

Warner refused to concede a point of doctrine and built a town over it. Twenty-seven years earlier, John Lothropp had refused to concede a point of doctrine and been gaoled for two years over it. What the town does next is what that kind of person does.

Four years after that first town meeting, two men arrived in Hadley in disguise.

Edward Whalley and his son-in-law William Goffe were regicides — among the commissioners who had signed the death warrant of Charles I in 1649. When the monarchy was restored in 1660 the new king's government hunted them across two continents. They fled to New England, were moved from safe house to safe house, and in the winter of 1664 they were taken to Hadley and hidden in the house of Rev. John Russell, the town's minister — the same man who had led the seceders out of Hartford.

They stayed for years. Whalley died there. The secret held for the rest of Russell's life and was not common knowledge until he died in 1692, nearly three decades later — in a town of a few hundred people, where everyone knew everyone's business.

Which is the point worth drawing out. Hadley existed because a group of Puritans had refused to bend on a question of baptism and would rather walk twenty-five miles into the wilderness than submit. Those are precisely the people you can trust to hide two men the King of England wants dead, and keep quiet about it for twenty-eight years. The stubbornness that founded the town is the stubbornness that concealed them.

Andrew Warner lived in Hadley the whole time. He died there in December 1684, with the secret still unbroken. In February 1679, in the middle of it, he is recorded taking the oath of allegiance to the Crown at Hadley.

And the part that is not true. Hadley's famous story is the Angel of Hadley: that during a King Philip's War attack in 1675 a white-bearded stranger with an old sword appeared from nowhere, rallied the town's militia, drove off the attackers and vanished, and that the stranger was Goffe, the old New Model Army major-general, breaking cover to save the town that sheltered him.

It is almost certainly invented. There is no record of an attack on Hadley that day. The tale seems to originate in a footnote in Governor Hutchinson's history of 1764 and was made famous by Ezra Stiles, President of Yale, in The History of the Three Judges. Goffe really was hiding in Hadley in 1675; the angel is a story people told afterwards.

It is kept here because the true version needs no help: two of the men who killed a king spent years concealed in a house in a town this family founded, and the neighbours never said a word. legend · not supported
A member of the family, Alice Williams (1924–2020), was admitted to the Daughters of the American Colonists on Andrew Warner's service.

The DAC is the quieter cousin of the better-known DAR, and it asks a different question. The DAR wants an ancestor who served in the Revolution. The DAC wants one who served the colonies before there was a revolution to serve — in civil office, in a militia, or in the plain work of establishing a settlement, any time between the first English landings and 1776. It exists to honour the people who built the thing the Revolution was later fought over.

So the qualifying service here is not a battle. It is founding towns.

King Philip's War · 1675–1678 documented

King Philip’s War, 1675

The Wampanoag had kept peace with Plymouth for half a century — it was the father of their sachem who made the first treaty with the people off the Mayflower. In 1675 that ended. The war that followed killed a greater share of New England's population than any conflict in American history since, the Revolution and the Civil War included. Twelve towns were destroyed outright. The colonists came close to losing.

John Carter of Woburn was twenty-one, and in Captain Nathaniel Davenport's company. That December they marched through frozen swamp in a snowstorm against a fortified Narragansett town built on an island in the mire — the Great Swamp Fight, one of the bloodiest single days in colonial American history. The attackers went in across a single fallen log. Davenport was shot dead at the head of his own company.

Carter came home. He lived another fifty-two years, made lieutenant, and lies in Woburn's First Burial Ground under a stone that still names the war.

The muster roll25 November – 3 December 1675

None of that is family memory. It is in George Madison Bodge's Soldiers in King Philip's War (1891), the standard register, compiled from the Massachusetts Archives, and it is specific. Bodge prints the lists of men impressed in the several towns where Captain Davenport's company was raised, returned between 25 November and 3 December 1675. The Woburn list runs to thirteen names. Carter is the first.

From Woburn. John Carter, William Johnson, James Convars, John Cutler, William Peirce, John Baker, Zachariah Snow, John Polly, John Preist, John Berbeane, John Shilden, Thomas Hale, John Bolen, Caleb Simons, Peter Bateman, Jerimiah Hood.Bodge, from Massachusetts Archives vol. 68

Two things follow from that. He was impressed — conscripted, not a volunteer. And three of the men standing beside him on the list came back wounded: Bodge's roll of Davenport's casualties names Caleb Symons, John Backer and Zachary Snow, all of Wooburne.

The list of that company's slain is headed by a single name — Capt. Nath: Davenport, and Bodge records where he was standing when it happened: “Capt. Mosely and Capt. Davenport led the van.” Carter's company went in first.

The night before18 December 1675

One of the men who was there wrote it down.

Dec. 18th we marched to Petaquamscot with all our forces, only a garrison left; that night was very stormy; we lay, one thousand, in the open field that long night. In the morning, Dec. 19th, Lord's day, at 5 o'clock we marched. Between 12 and 1 we came up with the enemy, and had a sore fight three hours. This is also the war the Angel of Hadley is supposed to belong to — the phantom soldier who saved Andrew Warner's town in 1675. He did not exist. The two regicides hidden a few doors away did.

Salem Village · 1692 · the fourth generation

Two sisters hanged at Salem, 1692

Rebecca and Mary Towne, daughters of William Towne and Joanna Blessing of Topsfield, were both executed in 1692. The descent runs through both of them, and through their brother Edmund Towne (1628–1678), who came from Great Yarmouth like his sisters, died at Topsfield fourteen years before the trials, and whose record carries 107 sources.

Rebecca Nurse was seventy-one, devout, and widely respected, which is exactly what made her case notorious. The jury returned not guilty. The accusers erupted; the judge invited the jury to reconsider a single ambiguous remark she had made; they came back with a conviction. Thirty-nine neighbours signed a petition on her behalf, which in that atmosphere was a dangerous act. She was hanged on 19 July 1692.

Her sister Mary Esty was hanged on 22 September, among the last eight. Knowing she would die, she petitioned the court — not for herself. She asked that no more innocent blood be shed, and proposed that the accusers be questioned separately and the confessors re-examined, because she was certain they were lying and would be found out. It is one of the most extraordinary documents of the whole episode. It changed nothing in time to save her, but the executions stopped that autumn.

A third sister, Sarah Cloyce, was accused, imprisoned, and survived.

The sisters come down opposite sides of this family

The descent from these two is not one line that happens to pass through a famous household. It is two lines, and they run down opposite halves of the record.

Rebecca Nurse descends to the maternal side — through her son John Francis Nurse, then Sarah Nurse, then four generations of Twisses, then the Hodges, ending at Alice Williams, who died in 2020. Mary Esty descends to the paternal side — through her son Isaac Eastey Jr, then the Cummings, the Porters, Lyman Beecher Warner, Hattie Mabel Warner, Beulah Lucretia Gore. Eleven generations one way, ten the other. They do not touch again until the last marriage in this record.

So two women hanged nine weeks apart in 1692 spent the next three hundred years travelling down separate branches of the same family — Massachusetts and Vermont and Iowa and Oregon on one side, New York and Pennsylvania on the other — and their descendants met in a hospital corridor in Portland in the 1970s without knowing it.

There is a third line. Their brother Edmund Towne (1628–1678), whose record carries 107 sources and who died fourteen years before the trials, also carries the descent, down the maternal side alongside Rebecca. That makes William Towne and Joanna Blessing ancestors three times over — through Rebecca, through Edmund, through Mary — which is more lines of descent than any other people named on this page. Ten others are ancestors twice; the Townes are the only ones who are ancestors three times, and the only ones anybody wrote a story about.

Two of the three children who carried this family forward were hanged as witches. The third died in his bed and is the reason the other two are not the whole of it. Where the two sides had already met sets out the other convergences.

The Towne family had a long land quarrel with the Putnams of Salem Village, who were central among the accusers. Historians generally treat that as material rather than coincidence. Spellings vary between Towne, Towen and Town, because that is how the clerks wrote it.

Part Two · 1624–1664 · 27 people

New Netherland

While the Puritans were filling Massachusetts, a second set of ancestors landed two hundred miles south, under a different flag, speaking a different language, and built the town that became New York.

II

Why they left1621–1624

The Dutch West India Company was chartered in 1621 to work the fur trade, and a trading post needs bodies. It found them among people who had nowhere else to be. When Spain reconquered the southern Netherlands, tens of thousands of French-speaking Protestants, Walloons, fled north and were living in Holland as refugees. The Company offered them land.

Utrecht, Amsterdam, Amersfoort, Hoorn, Arnhem, and Valenciennes in Hainaut, which is the tell. The first settlers put ashore in 1624 were not Dutch adventurers. They were exiles taking the only offer going, displaced a second time.

Ghislain de la Vigne and Adrienne Cuvellier, both born at Valenciennes, were among that first cohort in New Amsterdam; he was dead by 1632, in the settlement's opening years.

Wolfert Gerritse van Couwenhoven (1584–1662) came out from Amersfoort and helped establish the Brooklyn town of Flatlands — known then as New Amersfoort, named for the place he had left. Nicasius de Sille (1610–1673), a lawyer from Arnhem, arrived in 1653 as a councillor to Peter Stuyvesant and was among the founders of New Utrecht, where he died.

The English took the colony in 1664 without a shot; Stuyvesant surrendered rather than see the town burned. The Dutch families simply stayed and became English subjects, which is why these death places read Brooklyn, Bushwick, Flatbush, Flatlands and New Utrecht. Dutch villages that are now neighbourhoods, still carrying their names.

Part Three · 1709–1760 · 25 people

The Palatines

The winter of 1708 was the coldest in five hundred years. Wine froze in the barrel; birds fell out of the air. It came down on a Rhineland already wrecked by decades of French invasion, and it broke the region.

III

Why they left1708–1709

Around thirteen thousand people went down the Rhine to Rotterdam and on to London in 1709, camping in fields outside the city while Queen Anne's government decided what to do with them. Britain shipped roughly three thousand to New York to make tar and pitch for the Royal Navy.

The scheme failed within two years. The Palatines walked west into the Mohawk Valley and took the land instead. That is exactly where this part of the family ends up — Klock, Dygert, Fuchs, Etel and Riemensnyder all die at Palatine, Stone Arabia, German Flatts, Schoharie and Herkimer.

Which put them, two generations on, in the worst possible place. In August 1777 the Tryon County militia walked into an ambush at Oriskany — among the bloodiest engagements of the war by proportion of men lost, fought at close quarters against Loyalist neighbours and Iroquois allies.

Part Four · 1718–1775 · 12 people

The Ulster Scots

Scottish twice removed: Lowland Presbyterians planted in the north of Ireland in the 1600s, who left again a century later when Ireland turned out to be no better.

IV

Why they leftfrom 1717

The leases granted when their grandparents were planted in Ulster began falling in around 1717. Landlords raised the rent and re-let to the highest bidder — rack-renting, and families who had broken the ground could not meet it. The notorious case was the Marquis of Donegal's estate in County Antrim, where the evictions ran in the hundreds. Samuel Templeton was born in County Antrim in 1710.

On top of that: six consecutive years of drought, 1714 to 1719; the linen trade faltering; and the Test Act, which barred Presbyterians from civil office and still made them pay tithes to a church they did not attend.

They did not leave once. The sailings come in waves — 1717–18, 1725–29, 1740–41, 1754–55, 1771–75, and the 1740–41 wave follows a famine that killed perhaps four hundred thousand people in Ireland. Read together, the emigration tides are a chart of Ulster's economic health.

County Londonderry gave the Gilmores, County Donegal the Borlands, County Antrim the Templetons, County Down the Campbells.

Almost to a person they landed in Pennsylvania — Cumberland, Franklin, Westmoreland, Mercer and Armstrong counties, the Scots-Irish corridor running down the back of the Appalachians. The one exception proves it: William Jamison died at Windham, New Hampshire, next door to Londonderry, New Hampshire — a town founded in 1719 by emigrants from Ulster and named for the place they came from.

The sisters in the holdfamily tradition

The Gilmores were Covenanters — Presbyterians whose refusal to accept bishops or royal authority over the church had got them killed in Scotland and planted in Ireland.

The family story says Robert Gilmore and his brother were permitted to emigrate but their two sisters were not. So the brothers smuggled them aboard and hid them in the hold until the ship was far enough out to sea that turning back was not worth the captain's trouble.

No ship has been identified. Eighteenth-century passenger lists are patchy and the names are common. It is recorded here as tradition, but it belongs to a real migration, and the family it describes really did land in Pennsylvania and really did produce a Continental Marine.

1675–1945 · crossings I, III and IV

Nine who served, four wars

Two hundred and seventy years, four wars, and the same family. One fought the first full-scale war between colonists and Native nations; one drowned coming home from a failed invasion of Canada. Six are patriots of the Revolution. The last fought the Second World War. They come down from three different migrations, English Puritan, Rhineland German, Ulster Scot, and each is labelled with his war, because they are not the same thing and this page should not pretend otherwise.

The Northwest Territory · 1788–1795

The Ohio Company founds Marietta, 1788

This is the hinge. The single generational step where New England stops being where this family lives.

When the war ended, Congress had land but no money, and paid its veterans in western acres. The Ohio Company of Associates — largely New England officers and soldiers cashing in military warrants — founded Marietta in 1788, the first organised American settlement in the Northwest Territory. It was not a drift. It was a government finding a way to settle its debts, and it moved a whole generation of New England families over the mountains in twenty years.

Twenty-two ancestors died in Ohio between 1797 and 1861, and four of the same generation died in one Ohio county. Ebenezer Nye — the Connecticut soldier who had been paid continuously through Yorktown — went out to Marietta in 1790 and on to Rainbow in 1795, where he preached, held civil office, and donated the ground that became the village burial place. He was then buried in it. His sandstone marker still stands, and the Marietta DAR marked the grave.

My Saviour shall my life restore,
And raise me from my dark abode,
My flesh and soul shall part no more,
But dwell forever near my God. Ebenezer Nye, d. 27 February 1823 · Rainbow Cemetery, Ohio

Ebenezer Porter (1732–1827) of Ipswich was among the last of the party to reach Marietta in July 1788, and died at Little Hocking at ninety-four. Lydia Cummings and Solomon Fuller are buried in the same county.

You can watch that journey happen in the birthplaces of his fourteen children. Thirteen of them were born at Ipswich, Essex County, the last in January 1784. The next, Mary Polly, was born in New Jersey in March 1787. Sixteen months later the family was at Marietta. The family did not decide to go west so much as it was already moving.

The Revolution did not just end for this family. It relocated them, and everything west of here follows from it.

The northern frontier · 1750s–1791

The Republic of Vermont

Before the family went west, it went north — into land three colonies were arguing over, which solved the argument by declaring itself a country. Two men in this tree helped run it: one wrote its constitution and served as its Lieutenant Governor, and one kept the tavern where the whole thing was plotted. Only the first is a direct ancestor. The second married in, which is its own kind of fact about how a family ends up standing next to history.

Twenty-four ancestors died in Vermont, in records running from the 1740s to 1853: Halifax, Bennington, Guilford, Weathersfield, Hardwick. It was the cheap frontier for crowded Connecticut and Massachusetts families, and both New York and New Hampshire claimed it, having sold overlapping titles to the same acres.

In 1777 the settlers stopped waiting for a ruling and declared an independent republic. Vermont was its own country for fourteen years, with its own currency and its own postal service, until it joined the union in 1791. Several of these death records still carry the jurisdiction as it stood: Bennington, Republic of Vermont. Some ancestors here died as citizens of a country that no longer exists.

The man who kept the bar1766–1781

Not an ancestor — an uncle by marriage. Fay married Ruth Child, whose sister Prudence Child is the direct ancestor. The two women share the same parents, John Child and Hannah French, and the same nine siblings. So Fay is the husband of a sixth great-aunt: no blood, one marriage away, and in the room for all of it.

Capt. Stephen Fay Sr. (1715–1781) was an innkeeper in Westborough, Massachusetts, selectman four years, assessor five, innholder from 1754, and in 1766 he moved to Bennington and took over the Green Mountain Tavern, built three years earlier. History knows it by the sign that went up outside: a stuffed catamount mounted on a post, turned to face New York and grinning defiance at its claim to the land. The building carried that name for a century, until it burned in 1871. The Catamount Tavern.

It was not a bar that happened to have revolutionaries in it. It was the headquarters — of the Green Mountain Boys, of the fight with the New York land claimants, and then of the Revolution itself. Ethan Allen lived there, on and off, for years after 1766. When the settlers decided in 1772 to send men to negotiate directly with the royal governor of New York, they sent the landlord: Captain Fay and his son Jonas rode to confer with Governor Tryon.

Fay was sixty-two when the fighting reached his town. He did not carry a musket. He sent five sons, John, Elijah, Benjamin, Joseph and David, to the battle of Bennington on 16 August 1777. John did not come back. The family account records what happened when the news was brought to the tavern, and it is worth having in full:

“What! has he misbehaved?”

“No, sir. Worse than that — he is among the slain. He fell contending mightily in the cause.”

“Then I am satisfied,” replied the venerable sire; and in his deep grief, bowing his head, he exclaimed, “I thank God that I had a son who was willing to give his life for his country. Bring him in and lay him before me, that at my leisure I may behold and survey the darling of my soul.”

recorded of Stephen Fay, Bennington, August 1777

Supported — a nineteenth-century family memorial, not a contemporary document. The tavern, the 1772 mission and the five sons at Bennington are independently recorded; the exact words at the door are the part that rests on the memorial alone.

The man who wrote the constitution1777–1783

Benjamin Carpenter (1725–1804) came to Guilford in 1770 from Rhode Island, where he had been a magistrate. What he did next is carved on his gravestone, which reads less like an epitaph than a résumé:

“Hon. Benjamin Carpenter, Esq. … a field officer in the Revolutionary War, a founder of the first constitution and government of Vermont, a councilor of censors A.D. 1783, a member of the council and Lieutenant Governor of the state in A.D. 1779, left this world and 146 persons of lineal posterity March 29, 1804, aged 78 years, 10 months and 12 days.”

gravestone, Guilford, Vermont

That constitution is not a footnote. Vermont's, drafted in July 1777, was the first constitution in North America to abolish adult slavery, and the first to give every man the vote without requiring him to own property. A direct ancestor of this family was in the room.

He is seven generations back — closer than the Salem trials, closer than King Philip's War. And the last clause on the stone is doing something quietly enormous: 146 persons of lineal posterity. He counted. Everyone reading this page is in that number's continuation.

And Vermont is where the Gores were waiting

Amos Gore went up from Norwich, Connecticut to Halifax in the 1750s. His son Ebenezer was born there in 1793, and his grandson Emerson in 1824. Three generations in one Vermont town, and then the whole line picks up and leaves for Iowa, and after Iowa, Oregon.

And the two Vermont lines are the same line

These two men look like separate finds. They are not. Benjamin Carpenter's daughter Lydia married Amos Gore, the Gore who had come up to Halifax from Connecticut, and their son was Ebenezer Gore. Meanwhile Prudence Child, the sister Stephen Fay did not marry, had a daughter Mary Flagg, a grandson Elijah Haven, and a great-granddaughter Mary Polly Haven.

Ebenezer Gore married Mary Polly Haven. The Lieutenant Governor's grandson married the tavern keeper's great-grandniece, and every generation after that, Emerson, Edward, Beulah, carries both. The constitution and the barroom arrive in the same person. Both of them were born in Halifax, Vermont, four years apart.

And then the couple that joined the two lines did not stay together to the end of it. They went as far as Iowa. Ebenezer died at Charleston, in Lee County, in 1850 — the same year Congress passed the act that would give his son 640 acres of Oregon. Two years later Emerson left for the Rogue River Valley without his mother. Mary Polly Haven does not appear in Oregon at all. She died in 1872 at Morganville, in Clay County, Kansas, seventy-five years old and some fifteen hundred miles from the son who carried her line.

Unproven — both death places are in the tree and neither carries a narrative source; six records apiece, and nothing that explains how a Vermont-born widow reached central Kansas. It is the thinnest well-formed story on this page, and it is here because the alternative is to pretend the family went west together.

Stephen Fay's line did the same thing, in the other direction and a generation earlier. His widow Ruth Child outlived him by twenty-one years and did not die in Bennington — she died at Gallipolis, Ohio, in 1802, at eighty-seven. The Catamount Tavern is on the Vermont frontier in 1766 and the Ohio River in 1802. Same restlessness, one lifetime.

Place names in the record are normalised to modern jurisdictions, so “Republic of Vermont” is also applied to deaths before 1777, when no such republic existed. The label is the database's, not the clerk's — worth knowing before quoting one as evidence of anything.

Part Five · 1840–1870 · 8 people

The Industrial Crossing

Two hundred and twenty years after the Mayflower, with the family long since American, eight more people got on ships. They were not fleeing a bishop or a king. They were going where the work was.

V

Why they left1815–1843

This is the one crossing where the page had it backwards. They were not going where the work was. The work had gone.

Power looms began replacing hand weaving from about 1815, and over the next thirty years handloom weavers' incomes fell by roughly eighty per cent. Royal Commissions in 1834 and 1839 reported destitution. Then the trade collapsed outright: Paisley was declared bankrupt in 1843. Sixty-seven of its hundred and twelve mills failed, and 14,791 people were on relief in a town of about thirty-two thousand — near enough half of it.

The biographical sketch of Robert Linton says he was born at Paisley in 1843. Whichever of his two recorded birthplaces is right, Paisley or Kilmarnock in Ayrshire, both were weaving towns in the same collapse, and he was a small child when his parents put him on a ship. He did not choose this crossing. It was chosen for him by a machine.

Ireland sent Hugh Linton, Agnes Mitchell and Alexander Campbell. England sent one man.

They did not go to farmland. They went to Pawtucket and Providence, Rhode Island — the birthplace of American industry, where Samuel Slater had built the country's first water-powered cotton mill in 1793, and to Worcester, Massachusetts. The others went into coal and iron in Armstrong County, Pennsylvania. And Robert Linton made paper — forty years of it, and he was not merely in the trade, he was at the front of it.

The last man through the door1840–1917

Albert Mushen was born in England and died at Worcester, Massachusetts. He sits four generations back — recent enough to have been photographed, had anyone thought to.

And this is the part worth sitting with. Of the 560 people in this record who crossed an ocean, the one who carried the surname at the top of the page was among the last eight to arrive. Nine ancestors were already standing at Plymouth in 1620. Two hundred and twenty years of Warners, Gores, Nyes, Townes and Howlands had founded towns, been hanged, fought two wars and buried each other in New England ground before anyone in this family was called Mushen at all.

A surname travels down one thread out of thousands. On this page, the name is the newest thing in the building.

The inventors3 July 1849

Emerson and his twin brother Emery were cabinetmakers, and better with machinery than that suggests. Three years before they went west, the two of them were granted U.S. Patent No. 6,571 for a wind wheel — a vertical-shaft machine, two tiers of angled sails turning about an upright timber shaft, carried on a tapered eight-sided trestle and driving a toothed ring gear at the base. Most American windmills of the period turned on a horizontal axis like a Dutch mill. This one does not.

Twenty years later, in Oregon, Emerson patented a gang plough praised for being manageable “by a boy of twelve.” It was said the only real difference between the twins was that one was a Baptist and the other a Presbyterian.

The widow and the teacher1849

Mary Elizabeth Gilmore was born in Pennsylvania, granddaughter of the Covenanter migration and daughter of Robert Gilmore the Marine, which is how two of these five crossings finally meet in a single marriage. She lost her father young and her mother not long after. She had read the whole Bible before she was seven. They left Charleston, Iowa on 27 April 1852 in an ox-drawn train of about forty wagons and reached the Rogue River Valley on 27 September. Six months. Mary was pregnant the entire way.

The Gore Stockade1853

The land itself came from the Donation Land Claim Act, signed six weeks before they set out planning it: 640 acres to a married couple who reached Oregon Territory and worked it. And the Act did something almost no American law did in 1850 — it required the husband and wife to each hold half in their own name. Mary Elizabeth Gilmore Gore, who had been widowed at nineteen and taught school to feed herself, owned three hundred and twenty acres of Oregon outright, in an era when most married women in the United States could own nothing at all.

They arrived into a war. The Rogue River Wars were running, and in the summer of 1853 the Gores built a fort on that claim, at what is now the junction of Highway 99 and South Stage Road, south of Medford. It sheltered “settlers for miles around at times of Indian excitement,” with their families, stock and possessions. One recorded panic turned out to be men chasing a bear.

The drawing is not a picture of a building. It is a measurement, made by someone who wanted the thing recorded before the last of them died. On whether it was there at all. When the Historical Society printed this drawing in 1985 it ran without attribution, and the society afterwards distanced itself from any claim that the stockade had ever existed — there being no documentary evidence for it at the time. It was, in other words, exactly the kind of thing this page grades as family tradition.

It has since been substantiated. Later research located the notes the 1934 drawing was prepared from, and independent contemporary accounts describe families in the valley “forting up” that season, with three forts built and filled. The stockade stood on or near the site of today's Southern Oregon Nursery. A family story that was officially doubted for decades turns out to have been true, and specific.

The violin

The Gores farmed beside an Indigenous village and, unusually, got on with their neighbours. Mary nursed the sick and fed them. Emerson played the violin.

Then one day during the troubles an armed party came to the house. They did not attack. They came inside, sat down in a circle around Emerson, and the chief motioned for him to play.

So he played. He played until his arms gave out, and he did not stop, because he did not think he was permitted to stop, until the chief finally signalled that he could. Then they left.

Family memory holds that the music was part of what kept them safe. When the war came in 1854 the account records that the Indians “took up their luggage, went past Father's home, took the trail over Jacksonville hill” — passing the Gore place untouched, with every opportunity to do otherwise. Of Mary, the chief had said simply: she has a good heart.

The family's own record does not pretend this was innocence. It states plainly that the settlers were interlopers. What it claims is narrower and more credible: that these particular people behaved decently to their neighbours, and that it was remembered.

Afterwards

Early years were thin — six weeks living on beef without salt, salt at a dollar an ounce, seven dollars for a squash. Emerson farmed, did carpentry on the buildings of Jacksonville, and ran a sawmill on Bear Creek with Emery until the flood of 1861–62 carried it off. The family went on to orchards, livestock, shops, schools and banks. Walter, the boy born three months after the wagons stopped, became Medford's first school principal.

Mercer County, Pennsylvania · 1827–1844

Mary Gilmore’s childhood in Mercer County

Her father was a Covenanter who had smuggled his sisters out of Ireland, fought the Revolution, and was sixty-eight years old when she was born. He was dead before she could walk.

Robert Gilmore and his brother came out of the north of Ireland “of Scotch-Irish descent and Scottish Covenanters in religious belief, fleeing from persecution to gain religious liberty in the new world,” and settled in Mercer County, Pennsylvania. He was a blacksmith. He married Nancy Smith, who was some twenty-nine years his junior, and took her on a wedding journey of forty miles on horseback to a house she could not find — it was hidden behind a stump until they rode around it, and she was, the family says, dismayed.

Mary Elizabeth was born on 6 February 1827, when her father was sixty-eight and her nearest sibling was five years older. On 14 June 1828 he died of cancer of the face. She was sixteen months old. Whatever she knew of the stowaway crossing, Tun Tavern and Captain Mullan's Marines, she knew at second hand, from a house he was no longer in.

What she read

Because she was so much younger than the rest, she became her mother's constant companion, and her sweetest memories were of walking in the woods with her. She had two books. Her earliest textbooks were a single spelling book and the Bible, which she had read through before she was eight years old. A second account in the same family record puts it earlier still — that she had read the Bible in its entirety before she was seven.

In the yard stood a chestnut tree too large for her to reach anything on. So she sat underneath it and watched the squirrels drop the nuts, and ran and picked them up. It was, she wrote, her only way of getting those particular chestnuts.

Her sister Jane was four during the War of 1812 and could already read, which most of the pioneers could not. Jane sat on a bench in their father's blacksmith shop and read the newspaper aloud to the men who came in with work.

These stories are hers and her granddaughter's, from the family record. Mary taught school in Mercer County to earn the money for her own wedding. She had a number of very lovely calico dresses, and spun and wove her household linen. When Lewis Rose died in Iowa two years later, leaving her with a four-month-old son, she went back to teaching to support them both — walking the boy to her mother every morning and collecting him after school.

A correction to the family story. Mary is often described as having been orphaned. The record does not support it. Her father died when she was sixteen months old, but Nancy Smith lived until 1 January 1856 — twenty-eight years a widow, and still alive in Lee County, Iowa, when Mary drove west in 1852. The parting was not a death. Mary left her mother behind on the Iowa prairie at twenty-five, and never saw her again.

Crossing V · where the record becomes a photograph

Mary Elizabeth Gilmore

1827–1893 · Pennsylvania → Oregon · teacher

She is the join, and she is one generation from Ulster. Robert Gilmore — the brother who smuggled his two sisters aboard the ship in County Londonderry, and the private who enlisted in the Continental Marines seven weeks after the Declaration — was not her grandfather. He was her father. Same man, both stories. She was the youngest of his ten children, married at not quite eighteen, and widowed inside two years — then married Emerson Gore on 20 September 1849, three years to the day after her first husband died. She crossed two thousand miles of plain while pregnant, talked a stampeding ox team to a standstill, and is credited in the local histories as one of the first four white women to settle permanently in the Rogue Valley. She had ten children of her own — five boys and five girls.

She nursed her neighbours through the Rogue River Wars and was remembered by the people on the other side of that war as the woman with a good heart. When she was in the garden, the account says, they would come up to the house and look in at the windows to tease the children, who would scream and push the furniture against the door, to everyone's amusement but theirs.

In her last weeks she wrote her own history in pencil, a few words at a time, in the presence of her daughters Ida and Ella. It was transcribed thirty-three years later. It breaks off mid-sentence — “In 1857—”. Which is, as it happens, the year she did the thing her name is cast in bronze for.

Ireland · where the record burns

Why the Irish lines stop in 1789

The Gaelic Irish lines in this family do something none of the others do. They stop — abruptly, at births clustered between 1720 and 1821, half the depth of every other origin. English lines run twelve generations back. Irish lines die at six.

The reason is a fire. On 30 June 1922, at the opening of the Irish Civil War, the Public Record Office of Ireland burned in the Four Courts, taking the censuses of 1821 to 1851, half the Church of Ireland parish registers, and nearly every will and probate record — seven centuries of documents. The 1861 and 1871 censuses had already been pulped by the government; 1881 and 1891 went for paper salvage in the First World War. No Irish census survives intact before 1901.

Under the Penal Laws most Catholic parish registers do not begin until 1820 or later, so beneath the fire there is a second wall where there was never anything to burn.

Two Irish lines here do reach ten generations — Margaret Clark, born Londonderry 1692, and William Jamison, born 1697. They survive precisely because they left early: their descendants emigrated in the 1710s, so the families appear in American church books, land grants and probate, which never burned. The deepest Irish ancestry in this family is legible only where it stopped being Irish.

1850s–1978 · both sides of the family

Three musical lines, and where they converge

One thread runs the length of this record and does not stop. It starts in a cabin in the Rogue River Valley with a man playing until his arms give out, and it ends with a university band programme.

It is not one thread. It is three, running down three separate lines that had never met — one from Oregon, one from Ayrshire by way of a Rhode Island mill town, one from Ulster by way of western Pennsylvania. Each of them produced musicians for roughly a century before any of them married into the others.

First line · the Rogue River Valley1850s–1984

Emerson Elijah Gore (1824–1910) played the violin — for his neighbours, and on the day an armed party sat down in a circle in his house and the chief motioned for him to keep going.

His sons had a quartet, furnished music for occasions across the valley, and were active in all the church music. William H. Gore directed many church choirs.

Hattie Warner Gore (1873–1966) married Emerson's son Edward in 1899 and became the valley's musical institution in her own right. She taught piano in Medford for forty-three years, contributed to music publications while she taught, and wrote the concert reviews for the Medford Mail Tribune for twenty years. She represented the State Federation of Music Clubs locally and kept one of the largest private collections of concert programmes in the state, running back to 1882. She was also the first woman to serve on the Medford School Board, for three terms, and sang in the Presbyterian choir into old age.

Her daughter Beulah Lucretia Gore (1911–1984) also taught piano. Mother and daughter, the same instrument, the same trade.

Second line · Pawtucket1841–1919

Robert Linton, son of Hugh Linton (1804–1866) and Agnes Mitchell (1821–1879), was brought to America as a boy when his parents settled at Pawtucket. His father Hugh was born in Ireland around 1804, so this line runs Ulster, then Scotland, then Rhode Island: Scots planted in Ireland, back to Scotland, and gone again inside two generations. He is the one person in the last crossing who did not choose it. He was a child.

He went to the Pawtucket public schools, and when they were done with him he went into cardboard. He stayed an employee until 1870, learning every process, and then, at about twenty-seven, went into business with his brothers as Linton Brothers, coating paper at a plant on Front Street. The brothers are named on his memorial: James Linton (1840–1915) and Hugh Linton Jr (1846–1918), with a fourth partner, Edward Jollie. There was also a sister, Janette (1832–1885).

Read that again. Not the first in Pawtucket — the first. The firm went on to make what the sketch calls “a perfectly surfaced paper made especially for fine hair line effects required by modern printers in both lithograph and half-tone printing.”

Which gives the portrait above a second life. That picture is a half-tone. Look closely and you can see the screen. The man in it spent forty years making the paper that printing like it required, and the only surviving likeness of him reaches us on exactly that stock.

Seven living children

He married Elizabeth Smith (1846–1938) in 1870, the same year he founded the firm, and the sketch records them as “the parents of seven living children, three sons and four daughters.”

Living is doing quiet work in that sentence. The memorial records two more: Mabel Thompson Linton, born and died in 1876, and John Linton, born and died in 1878. Two babies, two years apart, neither of whom saw a first birthday. A biographical sketch written to describe a successful manufacturer had no room for them, and the gravestones did.

The eldest son, Robert Wilson Linton (1871–1949), went into the business with his father and succeeded him as its head in 1908, which is what the sketch means by a man “rounding out a long life of usefulness.” It was written while he was handing it over.

Third line · Cornell and Corvallis1947–1978

And from a line with no Gore blood, no Oregon and no Rhode Island, a Pennsylvania Campbell four generations out of County Down, William Alexander Campbell (1913–1999) was Director of Bands at Cornell for nineteen years and then chaired the Music Department at Oregon State.

Where they meet

Beulah Gore, the piano teacher, married Samuel Albert Mushen — Robert Linton's great-grandson. That is lines one and two, a Medford piano bench and a Pawtucket bandstand, joined in one marriage. Their son's marriage brought in line three.

Three families, three countries of origin, three centuries of arriving separately, and each of them, independently, kept turning out people who taught, conducted, reviewed, or led. Nobody was passing an instrument down; they had no contact until they married.

It is the one trait on this page that shows up in every line rather than travelling down one of them.

Oregon and Rhode Island · 1849–1910

The inventors, in two unconnected lines

The same thing that happened with music happened with machinery. Two families, three thousand miles apart, who had never met, and both of them spent the nineteenth century making things that had not existed before.

The Gore twins1849 & 1869

Cabinetmakers in Vermont, and better with machinery than that suggests. On 3 July 1849, three years before they went west, Emerson and Emery Gore were granted U.S. Patent No. 6,571 for a wind wheel — the drawing at the top of this page. A vertical-shaft machine, when almost every American windmill of the period turned on a horizontal axis like a Dutch mill.

Twenty years later, in Oregon, Emerson patented a gang plough praised for being manageable “by a boy of twelve.” The full account is with the Gores.

Robert Lintonfrom 1870

And in Pawtucket, at almost exactly the same time, a Scottish-born boy who had come up through the cardboard mills founded Linton Brothers and did something the trade had not managed:

…the first makers of a coated paper stock to stand printing and waterproofing tests.Biographical sketch of Robert Linton

They went on to a paper made for the fine hair-line effects that lithograph and half-tone printing demanded. And the sketch is careful about how he knew it — not a machine, but a method: his experience “enabled him to acquire a system that few paper manufacturers in the country possessed.”

Two different kinds of invention

That distinction is worth keeping. The Gores have a number. Patent 6,571 is a public document with drawings, filed and granted, and anyone can read it, which is exactly why it survives as the oldest image in this family's possession.

Linton has no number. No patent has been found in his name. What he had was process knowledge held inside a firm — the kind of invention that never becomes a document, only a product and a reputation, and that disappears completely when the people who knew it die. It is the commoner sort, and the sort family histories almost never manage to record.

Except here it did leave one trace, and you are looking at it further up: the only surviving portrait of Robert Linton is a half-tone — printed by the process his paper was made for.

Where the Campbell and Warner lines meet

Every crossing on this page is a line arriving separately. This is where the last two of them finally meet, in one marriage, in the twentieth century, and the man at the centre of it is the last of the eight who served.

What the marriage joins

He married Alice Louise Williams (1924–2020), the same woman who was admitted to the Daughters of the American Colonists on Andrew Warner's service, nine generations up.

Those two streams entered the country a hundred years and six hundred miles apart and had no reason ever to touch. Andrew Warner was laying out house lots at Hadley in 1660. The Campbells were still in County Down. It takes until the twentieth century, and these two people, for the lines to run into each other, and everything on this page is downstream of that.

And then, at a hospital in Portlandthe 1970s

The join above is a marriage. This is the geography that made it possible, and the second join that came out of it.

When Campbell took the Oregon State post in 1966 the family moved to Corvallis. Their daughter later came west to Oregon to be near them, and studied nursing at Oregon Health & Science University in Portland.

The other side of the family was already there. Beulah Lucretia Gore, the piano teacher, Hattie's daughter, and her husband Samuel Albert Mushen both died in Multnomah County. The Gores had come into the Rogue River Valley by ox wagon in 1852 and their descendants had drifted north to Portland over the following century. Their son read for his degree at Stanford and then came back north to medical school at OHSU.

Which is where the two of them met.

What actually arrived at that hospital

Everything on this page. One line had crossed on the Mayflower in 1620, founded Hadley over a quarrel about baptism, hidden two regicides, been hanged at Salem, fought at the Great Swamp and Oriskany and Yorktown, taken western acres at Marietta, waited three generations in Vermont, and driven an ox wagon into southern Oregon — arriving with a violin, a quartet, forty-three years of piano lessons and a Pawtucket bandstand behind it.

The other had been planted in Ulster, left County Down for Armstrong County, Pennsylvania, stayed there four generations, gone to war in the Pacific, and then crossed the continent one more time to run a music department — arriving from the opposite direction, three thousand miles and three hundred years later.

They met in a teaching hospital in Portland, which is the only place on the continent both of them had reached.

Their names are not on this page, and neither is anything else about them. Living people are omitted — the rule this record has kept from the start.

The war, and then the band room

He served in the United States Army in the Second World War. Then he spent thirty-one years running two university music programmes.

From 1947 to 1965 he was Director of Bands at Cornell — nineteen years, at the head of the Big Red Bands; Maurice Stith succeeded him in 1966. That was the year Campbell came west to Oregon State University, hired to lead the Music Department after Robert Walls stepped down. He chaired it from 1966 to 1978.

The university's own archives describe what he did there: Campbell “broadened the department's music curriculum and improved physical facilities, to include a music listening center.” In 1967 the department established Bachelor of Science and Bachelor of Arts degrees in Music, with three areas of concentration — applied music, music history and literature, and theory and composition. He handed the department to David Eiseman in 1978.

His service record is the one thing still unproved. That he served in the U.S. Army in the Second World War is family knowledge, and it is recorded here as that, because no service document has been produced for it. It sits at the newest end of the tree rather than the oldest, which is not where you expect to find a gap.

There may be a reason for that. On 12 July 1973 the National Personnel Records Center in St. Louis burned, and took with it roughly eighty per cent of United States Army personnel files for servicemen discharged between 1912 and 1960. No index, no microfilm, no duplicate. It is the same problem as the Four Courts in Dublin, fifty-one years later and on another continent: a single building, one fire, and a whole population becomes hard to prove.

What would settle it: a discharge certificate (the WWII-era WD AGO Form 53-55, or a later DD-214), an enlistment record, a unit photograph, a medal or ribbon bar, a burial flag, or a cemetery marker giving branch and unit. Read the third group and you can see a whole crossing

Which is the sentence the whole page has been circling. Read the diverging figure again: for three hundred years these are two records that happen to be bound together. The Palatine row has nothing on the left of the line at all.

And the name on the door is the thinnest line in the tree

One surname does run the whole way down: the one this page is named after. It is not on the chart above, because it does not clear the five-person bar the other names clear easily. There are six Mushens in a database of 24,263 people, and only four of them are far enough back to appear on this record at all.

That is not an accident of research. It is what a pedigree looks like: you inherit one surname and roughly a thousand families, and the further back you go the more of what you find arrives through mothers. The name on the door is the one line nobody had to go looking for, and it is the least documented thing here.

A note from the person who commissioned this

How this changed my view

I was taught history as a sequence of set pieces. The Mayflower. Plymouth Rock. The colonies. The Revolution. The Constitution. Each one arrives more or less finished, and the gaps between them are empty. Assembling four hundred years of one family turned out to be an argument against that, and it is the thing I did not expect to get out of it.

The Constitution was the part that always felt like a magic trick. Fifty-five men go into a room in Philadelphia in 1787 and come out holding the architecture of a republic, apparently from first principles. Where would you even start?

The Constitution was written in Philadelphia. Constitutional culture was not.

That distinction is the whole of what I am claiming, and it is worth being precise about, because the strong version is false. The document does not predate 1787. Nobody was carrying a draft around Massachusetts in 1650. What predates it are the institutional habits that made such a document writable at all — and those had been in continuous use, in this family, for six generations.

You would start, I now think, with a question rather than a principle: how do you organise collective life outside a king's chain of authority? And once you ask it that way, it stops being a question anyone answered in 1787. It is a question my ancestors had been answering, badly and repeatedly and at considerable personal cost, for about a hundred and fifty years before anyone called it constitutional.

One part of the Reformation unintentionally decentralised authority

That is the sentence the rest of this rests on, and it needs two qualifications before it will hold weight. The first is unintentionally: nobody set out to invent self-government. They set out to fix what they believed was wrong with the church, and decentralised authority was a by-product, for the most part an unwelcome one.

The second is that this is not true of the Reformation generally, and I would be overreaching to say it was. Lutheran state churches and the Church of England did not dismantle the chain of authority. They re-headed it. Cuius regio, eius religio — whose realm, his religion — hands the prince what the pope had. Henry VIII did not abolish the hierarchy; he put himself at the top of it.

The claim holds specifically for the Reformed and congregational traditions — the Separatists, Congregationalists, Presbyterians and Baptists who located authority in the gathered congregation and in the text rather than in an office. Which happens to be exactly, and narrowly, the tradition of the people in this record: Lothropp a Separatist, Warner a Congregationalist, the Gilmores out of the Scottish Covenanting church, Dudley a Massachusetts Congregationalist to his last breath.

And narrowing it that way sharpens the point rather than blunting it. The oath Lothropp went to prison for refusing was an oath acknowledging the re-headed chain. He would not swear the King was head of the church. The tradition this record comes from is defined by declining exactly the settlement that kept authority descending.

In the world these people were born into, the question who says? had a settled answer, and the answer was a chain:

GodKingBishopPriestyou

Every link guaranteed the next. No link had to justify itself locally, because its authority arrived from above.

Put scripture in the vernacular and hand it to ordinary believers, and you have not merely changed a doctrine. You have removed the requirement that authority descend. If a ploughman can read the text the priest reads, the priest's standing no longer rests on exclusive access, and the bishop above him no longer settles anything the ploughman cannot reopen. The chain does not get replaced by a better chain. It stops being a chain.

And every question the chain used to answer silently now has to be answered out loud, locally, by people who have no one to defer to. That is the whole transition. Not a new philosophy of liberty — an authority vacuum, and the ordinary human necessity of filling it before the next Sunday.

So congregations found themselves settling, by argument and vote and covenant, things no one had asked them to settle:

  • Who has legitimate authority — and does it come from office, tradition, consent, or the text?
  • Who belongs to this community, and who decides?
  • Who may speak, vote, preach, or hold office?
  • What happens to someone who dissents?
  • How much disagreement can a community absorb before it breaks?
  • Can several self-governing communities coexist under something larger without being swallowed by it?

Those are religious questions. They are also, word for word, constitutional questions — and a congregation that has answered them has, without meaning to, written a small constitution and lived under it.

Which is why the century and a half between the Reformation and Philadelphia is not an empty gap between set pieces. It is thousands of communities running the same experiment, badly, in parallel, and keeping written minutes.

This record is that sequence, with names on it

What changed my mind is that I did not have to go looking for examples. They were already on this page, three generations deep, in the order the argument requires.

1632. John Lothropp goes to prison in London for two years rather than swear that the King is head of the church. The trigger is not an abstraction: Charles I orders the Book of Sports read aloud from every pulpit, and Lothropp's congregation will not have the Crown telling them what Sunday is for. He is released on condition he leave the country, and in 1639 he leads his people to a place they name Barnstable. That is a congregation acting as a political body — negotiating with a state, losing, and relocating rather than submitting.

1659. Andrew Warner and about sixty others walk out of Hartford over a dispute about baptism and church membership — who counts, and whose children count — and found Hadley. A doctrinal disagreement produces a town. The mechanism by which seventeenth-century New England generated new institutions was schism.

And Thomas Dudley, who is the reason I cannot tell this as a story about liberty.

Dudley is the part that makes it true

Dudley left England as a dissenter and arrived in Massachusetts as the authority. He ruled against Anne Hutchinson in the Antinomian Controversy — the colony's direct test of whether a person could differ from its ministers and remain — and he was on the side that said no. He never softened. The verses reportedly found in his pocket when he died were an argument against religious toleration: a warning to men of God to watch for any who would “a toleration hatch,” lest the ill egg bring forth a cockatrice.

So within one family, in one generation, are a man imprisoned for refusing the established church and a man who governed an established church and thought tolerating dissent was the crowning sin. That is not a march from persecution to liberty. It is a long argument about whether freedom means freedom for us or freedom for people who reject us — and my ancestors were on both sides of it, sometimes in the same household.

The contradiction is not a flaw in the story. It is the mechanism. Religious toleration did not arrive because people became enlightened. It arrived because once several communities each claimed the freedom to establish the true order, no one sect could hold unchecked power without reproducing exactly the persecution some other sect had crossed an ocean to escape. Toleration was, first, a structural necessity discovered by people who would rather not have needed it.

The vocabulary changes before the structure does

Here is the thing I only saw after writing the rest of it down. Three words recur across this record, four hundred years apart, and I had been treating them as different subjects:

covenantcharterconstitution

Congregations write covenants. Colonies receive charters. States write constitutions. In every case a community is doing one thing: reducing its governing arrangements to writing, and then living under the document.

They are not three subjects. They are successive names for the same practice, applied at increasing scale, and the name changes long before the underlying habit does. A group of people agree what they are, who belongs, who decides, and what happens when someone breaks it — and then they write it down, precisely so that it does not depend on who is currently in charge.

That is why the leap to 1787 looks smaller from here than it did when I was taught it. The men in Philadelphia were not reaching for an unfamiliar instrument. They were using the only instrument their culture had ever used, on the largest thing anyone had yet tried it on.

Then it scales

A covenant governs a congregation. A congregation governs itself alongside others in a town. A town is one of several in a colony. A colony holds a charter. A state writes a constitution. And then the hard problem, the one Philadelphia actually faced:

How do you govern governments, without dissolving them and without recreating centralised sovereignty under a new name?the problem federalism exists to solve

Put that way, federalism stops being an abstraction imposed from above. It is an architecture for coordinating institutions that had already been governing themselves for six generations and had no intention of stopping.

And the reading habit is not incidental

The Protestant insistence on going to the text yourself produced a culture where ordinary people were expected to read an authoritative document, interpret it, compare claims, and argue from the text. That does not automatically produce a liberal democracy. It does produce habits unusually compatible with constitutional life: authority expressed in writing, legitimacy contested through interpretation, ordinary citizens expected to know the governing rules, disputes framed as arguments about the meaning of a shared text, and institutions bound by language that outlives whoever currently holds office.

There is a small version of this on this page. Mary Elizabeth Gilmore, orphaned young, read the Bible cover to cover at seven. In 1857 she helped found a church in a valley that barely had a road. Two hundred and twenty years after Lothropp went to prison, the same practice: a group of people, a written covenant, an institution built from nothing but agreement.

Seen from there, the Constitution reads as a secular covenant — which is not a metaphor I am reaching for, but the fourth word in the sequence above. A written instrument establishing a community, allocating powers, defining membership and procedure, and binding the governors as well as the governed.

And the Bill of Rights is the errata

The amendments read differently once you assume the people writing them had experience rather than theory. Freedom of religion and speech read against a long history of governments policing belief and its expression. The privilege against self-incrimination reads differently when set against centuries in which religious and political authorities could compel declarations of belief, allegiance and association. The limits on searches, standing armies, quartering and coercive prosecution read against accumulated experience of government reaching into ordinary life.

I am deliberately saying reads against rather than descends from. Lothropp went to prison in 1632 for refusing to swear an oath about what he believed; that is a fact about him, and it belongs in the same world as the Fifth Amendment. It is not a claim that one produced the other, and the honest historiography of that link is contested. What I am claiming is weaker and, I think, sturdier: the people who wrote these clauses were drawing on what had already happened to people like the ones in this record.

Not one clause descends cleanly from one Protestant practice; the line is far more tangled than that, and anywhere it looks clean I have probably oversimplified it. The point is the weaker one: the population had spent a hundred and fifty years building, contesting and revising institutions of authority before anyone sat down in Philadelphia, and that experience was in the room.

The compact version

Philadelphia wrote the Constitution. It did not invent the practice of constituting things. What it codified was roughly a century and a half of experiments in self-government that began when religious communities tried to live outside the king's ecclesiastical hierarchy. Congregations became miniature governments. Their disputes forced the questions of authority, consent, literacy, conscience, discipline and toleration. Towns and colonies scaled the answers upward. The Constitution became the framework for governing all those governments at once.

The reason I believe it is not that it is elegant. It is that I can put names to every step, and the names are in this record, and several of them were on the wrong side of the argument they were helping to invent.

One last thing, which I did not plan and only noticed at the end. In 1777, my fifth great-grandfather Benjamin Carpenter helped write a constitution — Vermont's, the first in North America to abolish adult slavery and the first to give every man the vote without requiring him to own property. Ten years before Philadelphia, and a hundred and forty-five years after Lothropp refused the oath, somebody in this family was in a room drafting the answer.

He was not a genius in a room. He was the sixth generation of people who had been practising.

Errata · provenance · the edges of this record

Where this stops, and what it stands on

This record begins with people getting onto ships. That is a choice about evidence, not about where the family begins — and it is worth being explicit about what sits on the far side of that line, who actually built the thing underneath all of this, and what both of us got wrong.

The Atlantic is where the record starts. It is not where the tree stops.

Every story on this page happens between 1620 and the present, because that is the span where the documents are. The database does not stop there. It runs back through twenty-six generations to a man recorded as Dunlaig II d'Ossory, born 785, and the further back it goes the less any of it can be checked.

The proportions are worth stating plainly. 18,019 of the 24,263 people in this tree — three quarters of it — sit at generation seventeen or deeper. In the generations this page actually writes about, the average person carries fourteen and a half sources. Past generation seventeen the average is 2.9, and 10,041 people in the tree carry no source at all. Nearly a thousand deaths are recorded in England and Scotland for every one in America.

That deep half is not nothing — some of it is real, well-attested medieval record. But it is also where the tree quietly becomes a copy of other trees: the same disputed Dudley descent, the same Plantagenet on-ramp, the same Cheshire gentry marrying each other eight ways. 7,943 couples were never walked to completion, so the honest statement about King John, or Somerled, or Robert the Bruce is not disproven. It is untraced, which is a different and less flattering word.

So the page ends where the evidence ends rather than where the names do. If it went further it would be repeating other people's homework and calling it descent.
