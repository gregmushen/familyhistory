Rejected

People who are not ancestors

Names that attach themselves to a tree because they are famous, or because the spelling matched.

William Brewster, died 1593

Rejected
The claim
That the family descends from Elder William Brewster, the senior religious figure of Plymouth Colony and one of the most sought-after Mayflower ancestors in America.
Where it came from
A William Brewster present in the collaborative tree, matched on name alone.
How it was ruled out
The candidate died in 1593. Elder Brewster lived until 1644 and sailed in 1620. The man in the tree was dead twenty-seven years before the ship left. Kinship on the main record is established from parent–child edges rather than name matching, which is what surfaced this.

George Soule, born a century and a half early

Rejected
The claim
Descent from George Soule, Mayflower passenger and signer of the Compact.
Where it came from
The same place — a same-named person in the tree.
How it was ruled out
Birth date roughly 150 years too early to have boarded anything in 1620. The nine passengers that did survive checking are all recognised by the General Society of Mayflower Descendants.

Robert Linton of Woonsocket

Rejected
The claim
That the Linton Block at Monument Square, Woonsocket — a four-storey Queen Anne and Romanesque Revival building put up in 1888 — was built by our Robert Linton.
Where it came from
Wikipedia, “Linton Block”, found while searching the surname.
How it was ruled out
Wrong town and wrong trade. That Robert Linton was a druggist in Woonsocket; ours was a paper manufacturer in Pawtucket, fourteen miles away. No connection has been established. The name was simply common in Rhode Island.

John Foxe, the martyrologist

Not published
The claim
A John Foxe sits at the fifteenth generation with 76 sources, born July 1516 at Boston, Lincolnshire and died 18 April 1587 in London. Those are the exact dates and places of the John Foxe — author of Foxe's Book of Martyrs, after the Bible the most-read book in Protestant England for two centuries, and the book that taught every Puritan on the main record what martyrdom looked like.
Where it came from
The FamilySearch collaborative tree.
Why it is not on the record
An exact four-point match is also exactly what a wishful attachment looks like. Hanging a famous man's vitals on a same-named ancestor is the single commonest failure mode in collaborative trees, and it happens most at the deep generations where nobody checks. The intervening generations would need proving first. It is a lead, not a finding.

Rejected

Impossible dates

Two deaths recorded in colonies that did not exist yet. Both were excluded from the westward-drift analysis on the main record.

A death in Massachusetts, 1605

Excluded
The claim
Ralph Rich Plumer, recorded as dying in Massachusetts in 1605 — which would make him the family's first American by fifteen years.
Where it came from
The collaborative tree, at the fourteenth generation.
How it was ruled out
Plymouth was founded in 1620 and Massachusetts Bay in 1630. There was no Massachusetts to die in. The record's first arrival remains November 1620.

A death in Connecticut, 1602

Excluded
The claim
Mrs. John Clark, recorded as dying in Connecticut in 1602.
Where it came from
The collaborative tree, at the twelfth generation.
How it was ruled out
The Connecticut Colony dates from 1636 — thirty-four years later. Bad dates cluster exactly where a tree is thinnest and nobody has checked, which is why both of these sit deep.

Doubtful

Faces that cannot be theirs

Costume dates a picture. Three of these were ruled out on the wig alone, and one on the shape of a dress.

The Lothropp badge

Not him
The claim
A commemorative badge captioned JOHN LATHROP, showing a clergyman between a sailing ship and a meeting house, circulated as a likeness of the Rev. John Lothropp (1584–1653).
Where it came from
Genealogy sites, attached to his profile.
How it was ruled out
The powdered wig and preaching bands are eighteenth-century — a hundred years after he died. There was also a later Rev. John Lathrop (1740–1816) of Boston, which is the likelier subject. It is used on the main record only with that caption.

The Dudley engraving

Not him
The claim
An engraved bust circulated as Gov. Thomas Dudley (1576–1653).
Where it came from
Genealogy sites.
How it was ruled out
A full-bottomed periwig — a fashion that arrived with Charles II after 1660, seven years after Dudley died. Second wig on the record to outlive its subject, and the pair is why costume is now the first thing checked on any supposed likeness here.

The Mayflower Society emblem

Not a portrait
The claim
Images presented as likenesses of Mayflower passengers.
Where it came from
Genealogy sites, which attach the identical file to five of the nine.
How it was ruled out
It is the General Society of Mayflower Descendants emblem, not anybody's face. No likeness of any of the nine survives. They were ordinary people; portraits were for the wealthy.

The Edwardian photograph of “Mary Gore”

Unidentified
The claim
A studio photograph of a young woman in a light floral dress, supplied from the family album as Mary Gore.
Where it came from
The family's own photographs.
How it was ruled out
The pompadour hair, the lace bertha over a pouter-pigeon bodice and the high gathered waist put it around 1905–1915. Mary Elizabeth Gilmore Gore died in 1893, and would have been near eighty by then in any case; the sitter is decades younger.
Who it may be
There are two Mary Elizabeth Gores. She named a daughter Mary Elizabeth Gore, born 30 October 1863, who would be in her forties in that window. A granddaughter is also possible. Held back rather than captioned wrongly.

Doubtful

Legends

The Angel of Hadley

Legend
The claim
That during a King Philip's War attack on Hadley in 1675 — the town Andrew Warner founded — a white-bearded stranger with an old sword appeared from nowhere, rallied the militia, drove off the attackers and vanished; and that he was William Goffe, the regicide hidden in the minister's house, breaking cover to save the town sheltering him.
Where it came from
A footnote in Governor Hutchinson's history of 1764, made famous by Ezra Stiles, President of Yale, in The History of the Three Judges.
How it was ruled out
There is no record of an attack on Hadley that day. The story appears more than eighty years after the fact and looks invented. What is true needs no help: Whalley and Goffe really were concealed there from the winter of 1664, and the secret held until Rev. John Russell died in 1692.

Unsettled

Records that disagree

Neither source is obviously the weaker. The main record carries both.

Robert Linton: Paisley 1843, or Kilmarnock 1841?

Unresolved
Source A
A biographical sketch published in his lifetime: born Paisley, Scotland, in 1843.
Source B
The family tree, with 41 sources on his record: Kilmarnock, Ayrshire, 30 May 1841.
The gap
A different town twenty miles off, and two years. Paisley is in Renfrewshire, Kilmarnock in Ayrshire. It is the ordinary condition of a nineteenth-century birth abroad: the man knew where he was born, and everyone writing it down afterwards was working from something he said.

Mary Gilmore and the Bible: seven, or eight?

Unresolved
Source A
“…had read the Bible in its entirety before she was 7 years of age.”
Source B
“Her earliest textbooks were a single spelling book and the Bible, which she had read through before she was eight years old.”
The gap
Both appear in the same family record, written by different hands. One year, and no way to choose. Both are given.

Van Dyke: J. D. or S. D.?

Unresolved
Source A
The 1857 church dedication tablet at Jacksonville names J. D. Van Dyke among the ten founding pioneers. Historic Jacksonville.
Source B
The 1934 drawing of the Gore stockade lists “S. D. Van Dyke, wife and son John G., 1852”. truwe.sohs.org.
The gap
One initial, between a handwritten roster and a cast bronze plaque. Almost certainly the same household — Keziah Van Dyke on the tablet was born Keziah Gilmore, Mary's sister — but the page does not assert the identity on one letter.

Hattie Warner Gore: died 1961 or 1966?

Resolved on arithmetic
Source A
Her obituary is transcribed as Medford Mail Tribune, 4 October 1961, and gives her age as 92.
Source B
The tree gives her dates as 1873–1966.
The gap
Born 31 October 1873, she would have been 87 in October 1961, not 92. Ninety-two puts it in 1966, which matches the tree. The main record uses 1966 and leaves the 1961 date out rather than reconcile somebody else's typo.

Unsettled

Claimed, not shown

Longstanding and specific, and still without the document that would settle them.

Ebenezer Porter's Revolutionary service

Unproven
The claim
That Ebenezer Porter (1732–1827) of Ipswich served in the Revolution. He is listed as the sixth patriot on the main record.
Where it came from
The family record and the Ohio Company material. He was among the last of that party into Marietta in July 1788 and died at Little Hocking at ninety-four.
All 42 sources have now been read
They break down as roughly twenty vital records — most of them the baptisms of his own children, where he appears as the father — nine Essex County land deeds, his father Nehemiah's probate, one Washington County (Ohio) deed index, a marriage, a Find a Grave entry and two legacy file stubs. Not one is a military record. No muster roll, no pay voucher, no pension file, no service certificate. The sources document a farmer who bought and sold land in Essex County, baptised fourteen children and went west.
What the reading did establish
He was in Ipswich for the whole war. His children were born there in an unbroken line — 1774, 1776, 1778, 1780, 1784 — so he was resident, of military age (forty-four in 1776), in the county that mobilised hardest in Massachusetts. That makes local militia service entirely plausible and still entirely undocumented. Essex County militia returns are where it would be, if it is anywhere.

The Nova Scotia sources on Ebenezer Porter

Wrong man
The claim
Two of the 42 sources attached to his profile place the family in Yarmouth, Nova Scotia: Edmund Duval Poole's Annals of Yarmouth and Barrington (Nova Scotia) in the Revolutionary War (1899), and a Yarmouth genealogy of the Nehemiah Porter family. Taken at face value they would put him on the British side of the line for the entire Revolution.
What the book actually contains
Genuine primary documents from the Massachusetts Archives. On 4 October 1776 the Council at Watertown granted safe conduct to “Ebenr Porter, Richard Valpey, Nathan Utley, Henry Coggin, Asa Hammond & Nehemiah Porter, all of Yarmouth, in ye Province of Nova Scotia” — New England settlers asking leave to bring their families home, and asking American privateers not to seize them on the way. A real and little-known story: the New England Planters caught in British territory when the war began.
How it was ruled out
It is a different Ebenezer Porter. The petitioner states he was “a native of Salem, where he was bro't up, and afterwards resided at Woburn” before removing to Yarmouth, and mentions a wife and five children. Ours was born at Ipswich, and his children were being born there continuously through those very years — 1774, 1776, 1778, 1780, 1784. He cannot have been in Nova Scotia; he was at home having children.
Why it matters
Two of his forty-two sources belong to someone else. Source counts measure attention, not accuracy — and a high count can carry a wrong-person attachment for years without anyone noticing.

William Campbell in the Philippines

Unproven
The claim
That William Alexander Campbell (1913–1999) served in the U.S. Army in the Second World War, in the Philippines.
Where it came from
The family record. Circumstantially, he took the Cornell Director of Bands post in 1947 — the first year the universities were filling again after demobilisation.
What is missing
Any service document. There may be a reason: on 12 July 1973 the National Personnel Records Center in St. Louis burned, destroying roughly eighty per cent of U.S. Army personnel files for men discharged between 1912 and 1960. No index, no microfilm, no duplicate. His Revolutionary counterparts are documented to pay vouchers; the twentieth-century soldier is the one with nothing.
What would settle it
A discharge certificate (WD AGO 53-55 or DD-214), an enlistment record, a unit photograph, a medal or ribbon bar, a burial flag, or a headstone giving branch and unit. Next of kin can also request a reconstruction from the NPRC, which rebuilds burned-block service from pay vouchers and morning reports.

Jesse Linton's relationship

Unestablished
The claim
That Jesse Linton, who follows Robert Linton in the Pawtucket Band's list of conductors, was his brother. This was briefly published on the main record and has been retracted.
Where it came from
Family understanding, and the adjacency of the two names in the conductor list.
How it was ruled out
Robert's siblings are named on his memorial — Janette, James and Hugh Jr. No Jesse. The biographical sketch independently names the brothers in the firm as Hugh and James, with Edward Jollie a fourth partner.
What is still open
He belongs to the family somewhere — a Jesse Linton is assistant treasurer of the Pawtucket Glazed Paper Company in 1917. He is not among the children recorded on the memorial either, though only four of nine appear there. Nephew or unlisted son both remain possible.

The Pawtucket Band conductor list

Single source
The claim
That a history of the Pawtucket Band, incorporated 1880, lists its conductors as William Gilmore, William H. Apelles, T. J. Allen, A. D. Harlow, Mr. Russell, Robert Linton and Jesse Linton.
Where it came from
A history of the band, supplied to this project.
What is missing
Independent corroboration. The Slater Trust Company's Pawtucket, Past and Present (1917) contains no mention of a band, an orchestra or a cornet band anywhere in it, and the Rhode Island brass-band histories reachable online do not cover Pawtucket. The Lintons are firmly placed in the town — four of them officer one paper company in 1917 — but that places the family, not the baton.
Where to look
The Pawtucket Public Library's digitised newspapers, 1825–1960. A town band changing conductors gets reported.

Anything on this page can move to the main record the moment a document turns up. That is the point of writing it down.
